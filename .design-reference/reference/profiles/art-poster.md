# Art and Cultural Posters

The following is a general reference for this genre; user requirements, attachments, and the actual context take precedence.

Applies to art, culture, exhibition, and experimental posters — works whose primary value lies in concept, mood, or visual language.

**Benchmarks**: cultural posters featured in Tokyo TDC, New York ADC, D&AD, the Warsaw International Poster Biennale, and similar selections, plus representative works by designers and studios such as Experimental Jetset, Paula Scher, Kenya Hara, and Ikko Tanaka.

## Goals

### Establish one dominant visual concept
Distill a single core visual gesture from the work's theme, cultural context, era, materials, or language. Typography, imagery, color, composition, and texture all serve this concept; avoid sampling a little of every style and losing your stance.

### Let type and image become expression together
An art poster may use giant type, unconventional layout, extreme cropping, text–image overlap, or type-as-graphic treatment, but these treatments must generate meaning and tension rather than merely pursue strangeness. Essential names, credits, dates, and venues must remain accurate and legible.

### Build authentic craft and materiality
Textures such as paper, ink, halftone dots, scan artifacts, collage, hand-cut edges, and digital glitch should emerge from the theme and run consistently through the entire piece. Avoid casually layering noise over a finished image to fake a sense of craft.

### Process imagery in depth
Art posters usually require re-creating imagery rather than placing the original image as-is. Obtain assets through search or generation, then build a visual language using background removal, duotone overprinting, edge extraction, pixelation, halftoning, multiple exposure, and unconventional cropping; use `pptd.md` for accurate text and editable composition, and Python or ImageMagick for complex image effects.

## Visual references

The following directions come from visual positions that have proven valid in cultural exhibitions, theater, film, publishing, and experimental posters. First distill a conceptual gesture from the subject, then choose materials and typography that fit it; even when using the same collage or handwriting techniques, different themes must produce different compositions.

The items below exist only to help you understand what excellent design in this genre can look like — they are not a routing table to be matched item by item, nor is any one of them required to be applied wholesale. Form your own direction from the user's content, then borrow composition, typography, color, imagery, or material treatment as needed; reasonable approaches not listed here are also acceptable.

### 01. Eastern Ink and Archival Narrative

**Applies to**: traditional art, literature, historical figures, heritage sites, and cultural exhibitions. Use a piece of calligraphy or painting, a landscape, an artifact, or a historical archive as the key visual, preserving the authentic layers of paper, ink tone, and seals; organize modern titles with a restrained grid, and use vertical typesetting, inscription labels, or small text only when culturally grounded. **Production**: prioritize searching for trustworthy images of original works and artifacts and cite their sources; images may be locally cropped and unified to a single tone; do not mix symbols from multiple dynasties or substitute an antique-look filter for real material.

### 02. Dialect and Local Letterform Posters

**Applies to**: urban culture, language, neighborhoods, folk memory, and local exhibitions. Extract letterform traits from dialect tones, shop signs, street signs, handwriting, or local printing, and let a single word or short phrase become the visual subject; keep supporting information modern and clear, creating weight and scale contrast against the main characters. **Production**: the main title may be customized or hand-drawn, with a small amount of streetscape, tickets, or print traces added to the background; verify the text is accurate first — do not turn "local character" into random old font libraries.

### 03. Documentary Photography Intervention

**Applies to**: people, social issues, the body, urban scenes, and contemporary art. Use a documentary photograph containing real relationships or actions, and intervene by painting over it, circling, blocking, repeated exposure, or one giant handwritten gesture; the photograph carries fact and emotion, while the intervention layer makes the argument — the two must not cancel each other out. **Production**: photographs should come first from real archives or licensed assets, with intervention colors limited to 1–2; do not obscure faces, event cues, and essential captions all at once.

### 04. Fabric and Material Collage

**Applies to**: textiles, handicraft, the body, rivers, memory, and material-themed exhibitions. Treat cloth, yarn, torn paper, rubbings, or worn edges as genuine compositional units, building direction through overlapping, stitching, and rupture; the title may run along fabric edges or gaps, with colors taken from the real materials. **Production**: prioritize collecting or searching for high-resolution materials, then cut them out while preserving fiber edges and shadows; do not pass off tiled digital textures as handwork, and do not mix in too many unrelated stickers.

### 05. Newspaper and Archival Layout

**Applies to**: historical retrospectives, labor, social observation, annual events, and research-based exhibitions. Build a sense of newsroom immediacy with masthead-style titles, narrow-column body text, numbering, timestamps, and one high-contrast photograph, then form a contemporary viewpoint through column-spanning large type, red annotations, or layout ruptures; information density is high, but hierarchy must stay clear. **Production**: use real text or verified excerpts, keep the layout editable, and treat images with black-and-white, duotone, and coarse halftone; do not fill space with unreadable placeholder text.

### 06. Stage and Film Experimental Collage

**Applies to**: theater, film festivals, dance, experimental music, and performance art. Around character conflict, stage movement, or film imagery, combine 2–4 photographs, silhouettes, and text fragments with clearly different scales, using occlusion and diagonal momentum to create dramatic tension; the film or play title must hold the single dominant position. **Production**: establish the narrative center first, then apply background removal, duotone overprinting, tearing, and grain; keep performer and production assets authentic; do not pile unrelated photos into an "artistic" collage.

### 07. Conceptual Objects and Surreal Scale

**Applies to**: conceptual art, architecture, technology, environment, and exhibitions with philosophical propositions. Choose an object related to the theme and accomplish a single visual proposition through magnification, levitation, absence, mirroring, or impossible space; keep the background as simple as possible so the title, object, and negative space form a three-way relationship. **Production**: you may generate or composite the scene first, then lay out the title and information separately in `pptd.md`; the surreal gesture should be explainable in one sentence — do not create meaningless spectacle.

### 08. Multi-Color Block Printing and Independent Press

**Applies to**: music, poetry, community, independent publishing, folklore, and experimental exhibitions. Use 2–3 spot colors, coarse halftones, uneven inking, and controlled registration drift to create a strong printed-paper feel, first flattening figures or objects into clear black-and-white shapes; the title may join the overprinting, but essential information stays at stable edges. **Production**: build the color separations first and check each color's independent form, then layer paper and ink traces; do not simply overlay noise on a finished image, and do not let every detail blur into grain.

### 09. Poetic Micro-Edited Zine

**Applies to**: poetry, memory, independent publishing, sound, diaries, and quiet conceptual themes. Keep 70%–90% of the canvas legible as empty paper, placing only one small visual event occupying 8%–25% — for example, two fragments almost touching, an interrupted line, or an isolated specimen; use a set of high-purity, small-area spot colors, typewriter/thin-serif short phrases, and visible paper fiber. **Production**: fix the paper surface, the attention ratio, and the single metaphor, then vary anchor position, carrier, and text distribution; do not turn the whitespace into accidental emptiness, and do not expand it into a commercial advertisement or a full scene.

### 10. Macro Photography and Giant Letterforms

**Applies to**: the body, materials, the senses, fashion, experimental imagery, and abstract themes. Magnify a patch of skin, fabric, artifact, or natural surface until it is cropped by two to three canvas edges, with the subject and one extremely short giant word occluding each other; use a single high-purity color field, wide outer margins, and a small amount of technical micro-text, with grain serving only to unify the photography and letterforms. **Production**: first choose high-resolution material that can withstand extreme cropping, then design the text–image interlock; do not simply enlarge low-quality images, and do not let the giant type have no semantic relation to the theme.

## Prohibited items
1. Do not treat piles of random textures, decorative shapes, and novelty fonts as a visual concept.
2. Do not copy a designer's or a reference work's composition, palette, or signature language without reason.
3. Do not use experimental typography to cover up errors in key information, missing text, or illegibility.
4. Do not use multiple competing key visual gestures at once, leaving the work's character ambiguous.
