# Invitations and Cards

The following is a general reference for this genre; user requirements, attachments, and the actual scenario take precedence.

Applies to wedding invitations, event invitations, business cards, certificates, and other small-format materials with identity, commemorative, or ceremonial attributes.

**Benchmarks**: outstanding wedding and cultural-event invitations, museum and brand invitations, professional certificate and business-card systems, and the work of established stationery studios in typography, paper, printing, and small-format information design.

## Goals

### Make information accurate and ceremonial
Names, the event, date, time, venue, identity, and contact details are the core of the deliverable and must be accurate and complete. On that foundation, use typography, a sense of paper, decoration, and whitespace to convey the distinct character of a wedding, celebration, business, cultural event, or commemorative occasion.

### Design for small sizes and real media
Invitations, business cards, and certificates are usually read up close, and may also be sent by phone or printed. Check font size, contrast, and safe margins in on-screen thumbnails and at actual size; do not judge the result only on a zoomed-in editing canvas.

### Control the relationship between decoration and text
Decorative motifs, borders, floral patterns, illustrations, and materials may only help establish a sense of occasion; they must not occupy the space of key information. Typography may have personality, but body text, dates, and addresses must remain clear; do not manufacture a false sense of refinement with multiple script faces.

### Produce accurate text and visual assets separately
Prioritize editable text in `pptd.md` for names, dates, and venues; patterns, florals, scene illustrations, and paper textures may be searched for, generated, or edited. Print materials may suggest embossing, foil stamping, or specialty paper effects, but when production parameters are not provided these serve only as visual mockups.

## Visual references

The following directions serve different tasks: invitation, identity exchange, and commemorative certification. First determine the delivery method, actual size, degree of formality, and the information that must appear, then choose one set of typography, paper, and decoration relationships; visual effects should strengthen the sense of ceremony and must not reduce the accuracy of names, dates, and venues.

The following entries are only meant to help you understand what excellent design in this genre might look like; they are not a routing table to be matched item by item, and you are not required to apply any one of them in full. Form your own direction from the user's content, then draw on their composition, typography, color, imagery, or material treatment as needed; reasonable approaches not listed here are also acceptable.

### 01. Contemporary editorial invitation

**Applies to**: brand launches, exhibition openings, dinners, forums, and formal cultural events. Establish order with an asymmetric grid, one large-scale date or event name, and generous but aligned whitespace; group body content by time, venue, dress code, and RSVP. Use one neutral typeface paired with one restrained display face, and keep color to black, white, and one brand color. **Production**: front, back, or unfolded panels share the grid; check the phone version and the print version separately. Do not equate formality with gold, script faces, and text too thin to read.

### 02. Seasonal botanical invitation

**Applies to**: weddings, anniversaries, garden parties, seasonal-term events, and nature-themed celebrations. Choose 1–3 plants related to the season, location, or people involved, and use watercolor, pressed flowers, line drawing, or real botanical elements to partially enclose the information area; names and date form the primary hierarchy, and the floral palette stays within a natural, low-saturation range. **Production**: keep botanical assets in one consistent art style and lighting, and reserve a print-safe zone at the edges; decoration must not reach into the address and time, and do not use generic wreaths unrelated to the season.

### 03. Documentary photography with signature lettering

**Applies to**: weddings, graduations, commemorative reunions, personal celebrations, and person-themed invitations. Use one authentic photo of a person or place to establish emotion; set the title or names in a controlled signature-style hand, and use a clear printed typeface for all other information; the photo may run full-bleed or occupy one side, with key information resting on stable whitespace or a separate color block. **Production**: prioritize the user's photos and keep people true to life; when necessary, unify them only through color grading, cropping, and light grain. Do not generate lookalike people, and do not let script faces carry addresses and times.

### 04. Paper, ink, and cultural inscriptions

**Applies to**: traditional festivals, tea gatherings, calligraphy, cultural salons, commemorative ceremonies, and East Asian stationery. Build one clear cultural thread from real paper fiber, ink marks, seals, or inscribed-label structures; set modern body text on a grid, and reserve vertical typesetting for short titles or names only; keep color to paper white, ink black, and one seal color. **Production**: seals, motifs, and calligraphy must have a contextual source, and accurate text is typeset independently. Do not pile on pseudo-antique borders and mismatched calligraphy styles, and do not substitute an aged-look filter for a real paper relationship.

### 05. Casual party and family cards

**Applies to**: birthdays, children's activities, community gatherings, friends' parties, and relaxed celebrations. Use a set of hand-drawn figures, food, gifts, or activity props around a large title, with bright color held to 4–6 hues; concentrate time, venue, and how to participate in a quiet area, and keep the theme legible in a phone thumbnail. **Production**: establish a uniform line weight and cast of characters first, then compose the decoration. Do not stuff every gap with stickers, and do not let playfulness turn into misspellings or sloppy information.

### 06. Professional business and identity cards

**Applies to**: personal business cards, brand staff cards, guest passes, membership cards, and event badges. Name and role form the first tier; organization, contact details, and any necessary numbers form the second; the logo and color blocks establish recognition. Front and back, or a card-and-sleeve system, share one grid; portraits, QR codes, and access information appear only when the business requires them. **Production**: at actual size, check minimum font size, bleed, and safe margins, and preserve the quiet zone around QR codes. Do not turn a business card into a shrunken poster, and do not fill in contact details yourself.

### 07. Ceremonial certificates and commemorative credentials

**Applies to**: certificates, award citations, commemorative cards, completion proofs, and formal letters of thanks. Use the issuing organization's name, recipient, subject, date, and signature/seal to form a centered or axial order; borders, emblems, and guilloche backgrounds carry only the atmosphere of authority and anti-counterfeiting. Names and the conferred matter have a clear primary-secondary relationship, and body text should not overuse script faces. **Production**: keep accurate fields editable and reserve space for signature/seal; when showing foil stamping or embossing, note that it is only an effect mockup. Do not fabricate certifying bodies, numbers, or signatures.

### 08. Ticket and pass systems

**Applies to**: performances, exhibitions, forums, film festivals, sporting events, and invitations that require check-in or zoning. Organize the event name, date, session, seat/zone, entry rules, and QR code in the order of a real ticket check, and use tear lines, numbering, color bands, or punch holes to build a ticket language; the main ticket and stub share information, but the stub keeps only what verification requires. **Production**: at actual size, check the QR code's quiet zone and minimum font size, and use stable color coding for VIP, media, guest, and other identities. Do not generate unscannable decorative QR codes or fabricate seats.

### 09. Folded leaflets, sleeve sets, and coordinated stationery

**Applies to**: weddings, brand dinners, major launches, membership privileges, and invitations that call for a physical unboxing experience. Treat the outer sleeve, main card, schedule card, map card, and reply card as one system: fix the paper color, typography, and identifying motif, then differentiate uses through size, folding, or spot colors; the opening sequence should match the reading order of the information. **Production**: draw the flat layout and enclosure relationships first, then produce mockups of paper, embossing, or foil effects; without die-cut and process parameters, do not claim the work is production-ready.

### 10. Digital animated invitation cards

**Applies to**: online launches, digital wedding invitations, countdowns, community events, and invitations meant for phone sharing. Use a static first frame so the event name, date, and venue are instantly readable; motion serves only to reveal the title, unfold a path, introduce subtle material changes, or run a countdown, with the loop contained in one clear action; the ending returns to the full-information page. **Production**: keep text and logo independent; prefer native PPT animation, and render complex paper, light-and-shadow, or particle effects as short GIFs with Python. Do not let motion obscure information or cause flicker fatigue.

## Prohibited items
1. Do not omit, rewrite, or generate incorrect names, dates, venues, contact details, or identity information.
2. Do not default every invitation to a wedding, luxury, gold, or script-face style.
3. Do not harm small-size readability with overly thin text, low-contrast colors, or overly dense decoration.
4. Do not present material and print-effect mockups as confirmed production processes or deliverable print files.
