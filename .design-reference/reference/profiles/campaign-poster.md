# Campaign Posters and Key Visuals

The following is a general reference for this genre; user requirements, attachments, and the actual scenario take precedence.

Applies to posters for events, recruitment, promotions, and launches, as well as roll-up banners, display boards, event backdrops, and wayfinding materials extended from the same key visual.

**Benchmarks**: outstanding campaign communication work collected by D&AD, Cannes Lions, AIGA, and similar institutions; key visuals and cross-format extension systems from established brand launches, music festivals, exhibitions, sporting events, and public events.

## Goals

### Capture the event theme with a single key visual
Campaign posters are typically an image-led genre. At least one strong visual focal point must exist among the main image, headline, or core graphic, and it must relate directly to the event's content, audience, and temperament; generic assets cannot substitute for expressing the theme.

### Convey actionable information fast
Viewers should be able to quickly identify what the event is, why it is worth attention, when and where it happens, and how to participate. Organize the theme, selling points, time, venue, organizer, and participation entry point by importance; do not give all information equal weight.

### Build an extensible key visual system
The primary colors, typography, core graphics, image treatment, and identity motif should extend to landscape formats, portrait formats, roll-up banners, display boards, and backdrops. Materials viewed from a distance should carry less information and larger identity elements, rather than being direct scale-ups of the original poster.

### Make full use of image search, generation, and editing
First determine whether real people, venues, products, or custom visuals are needed: real content suits searching and user-provided assets; conceptual scenes suit generation; before entering the layout, perform cropping, background removal, color grading, masking, and compositing. Keep headlines and event information editable using `pptd.md`; complex collages, halftones, and special image effects may be handled with Python or ImageMagick.

## Visual references

The following directions are organized around "how an event is remembered and joined," not as a generic list of poster styles. First select a key visual mechanism based on the event's attributes, then embed the headline, time, venue, organizer, and participation entry point into that mechanism; when extending to landscape, portrait, and on-site materials, retain the same core assets.

The following items only help illustrate what excellent design in this genre may look like; they are not a routing table to be matched item by item, nor is any single one required to be applied in full. Form your own direction from the user's content, then borrow from these compositions, typography, colors, imagery, or material treatments as needed; reasonable approaches not listed here are also acceptable.

### 01. City Festival Illustration Key Visual

**Applies to**: coffee festivals, markets, city culture festivals, tourism promotion, and local celebrations. Compose a roamable city illustration from landmarks, stalls, people, and representative objects; bring the headline into the scene as a building signboard, an archway, or a large shape at the top of the frame; draw the palette from the location or festival food, and fix the information block at the bottom or side. **Production**: search images first to verify real landmarks, then generate or draw illustration assets from the same viewpoint; do not replace a specific place with a set of generic city icons.

### 02. Single-Object Call-to-Action Poster

**Applies to**: lectures, reading sessions, environmental actions, recruitment, charity causes, and small themed events. Choose one chair, book, cup, door, tool, or natural object directly related to the action; let it occupy 40%–70% of the frame, then create a memory point through scale, color, or a slight surreal treatment; build a clear two-level hierarchy of headline and date around the object. **Production**: prefer real object photos with background removal, recoloring, or partial redrawing, and keep text editable; the metaphor must explain the event's content, not merely be strange.

### 03. Documentary Portraits with Handwritten Intervention

**Applies to**: theater, community events, personal story sharing, social issues, bands, and youth events. Use a photograph of people with genuine emotion and relationships as a full-bleed or large-area main image, then run a set of thick-pen handwriting, circling, or smearing across the frame to create a sense of presence; make the event name prominent, and place the time and venue in a stable dark area of the photo or on a separate color band. **Production**: prefer licensed real photography for people; draw the handwritten layer separately and limit it to one or two colors; do not let the strokes cover faces or essential information.

### 04. Map-and-Route Outdoor Key Visual

**Applies to**: city walks, hiking, camps, outdoor experiences, expo navigation, and route-based events. Use a real map, terrain, or simplified street grid as the base; connect start and finish points, supply stops, and key events with a high-contrast route, and compose the headline together with the route's shape; distance, time, difficulty, and meeting point form a separate information band. **Production**: the base map and route must be verifiable; landmark illustrations keep consistent scale and line weight; do not move locations or omit risk warnings for visual effect.

### 05. Craft-and-Material Cultural Events

**Applies to**: textiles, ceramics, intangible cultural heritage, food and drink, calligraphy, paper goods, and traditional culture events. Use woven texture, paper edges, embroidery, seals, brushstrokes, or vessel details as the key visual material; preserve hand-cut edges and tactility in the composition while organizing the event name and date with a modern grid; take colors from the real materials — do not default to gold and antique brown. **Production**: prefer photographing or searching for real craft details, then collage and separate colors; cultural symbols must have specific origins — do not mix unrelated traditional elements.

### 06. Youth Color Blocks and Symbol System

**Applies to**: campus, new consumer brands, creative communities, design weeks, trendy pop-ups, and light entertainment events. Establish long-distance recognition with one high-saturation background color and 1–3 large-scale symbols; keep only a few clearly outlined people or objects; set the headline in a bold weight or custom letterforms, and consolidate event information into one quiet zone. **Production**: use `pptd.md` to build extensible rules for color blocks, symbols, and typography, and reuse the same motif for on-site wayfinding; lively does not mean covered in stickers and random colors.

### 07. Cinematic Stage and Performance

**Applies to**: concerts, theater, dance, film festivals, nighttime events, and artist performances. Create depth with photographs of performers on stage, lighting, or the environment; use high-contrast shadows, a single stage color, and rhythmically arranged headlines; the artist, work title, or performance theme sits at the top level, with ticketing, showtimes, and venue immediately after. **Production**: search for or use real artist and venue assets; unify them into duotone, grain, or motion blur when necessary; do not generate people who are similar but wrong, and do not let atmosphere swallow the ticketing information.

### 08. Community Market Hand-Drawn Notice

**Applies to**: community markets, parent-child activities, reading clubs, breakfast meetups, small sales exhibitions, and charity gatherings. Surround a relaxed scene with hand-drawn stalls, food, people, or activity props; make the headline read like an on-site signboard; set the date and venue in clear printed type rather than handwriting everything; build warmth with a paper-toned background and 3–5 natural colors. **Production**: first draw a set of objects with uniform line weight, then assemble the layout; the handmade feel should retain order — do not stuff every gap with small graphics.

### 09. Motion-Blur Giant-Type Key Visual

**Applies to**: running, dance, live music, youth communities, and events that need a strong sense of speed. Use a full-bleed action photograph; give the subject a controlled trailing blur along its real direction of motion while preserving its outline; press a set of ultra-narrow giant type directly into the photo, overlapping the figure; let the primary color occupy most of the frame, and complete the contrast with one fluorescent spot color and a small amount of white. **Production**: lock the subject and actionable information first, then apply directional blur, spot-color mapping, and print grain; do not let the blur destroy the person's identity, and do not fake speed with an ordinary gradient.

### 10. Photography Overlaid with Flat Character Art

**Applies to**: city events, tourism festivals, campus, parent-child, pet events, and any event where a real location must coexist with an imagined character. Use a real venue or streetscape photo as the base; overlay an original flat character, animal, or symbolic figure occupying 30%–55% of the frame, with clear occlusion between the character and building edges; arrange the headline, date, and venue along empty areas in the photo or in a fixed sidebar. **Production**: unify the photo's color grading first; give the character a stable stroke and limited palette, adding a few hand-drawn arrows if needed; do not borrow existing IP, and do not weaken the protagonist with a swarm of small stickers.

## Prohibited items
1. Do not create only atmosphere while omitting or weakening essential information such as time, venue, target audience, and participation entry point.
2. Do not fill the canvas with generic stock imagery unrelated to the event, decorative icons, or templated cards.
3. Do not place text over low-contrast areas of an image, or use type sizes so small that the text is unreadable at the actual viewing distance.
4. Do not change the core figures, logo, colors, or identity motif when extending the same key visual to different materials.
