# Brand & Identity

The following is a general reference for this genre; user requirements, attachments, and the actual scenario take precedence.

Applies to logos, wordmarks, app icons, logo lockups, and foundational brand visual identity.

**Benchmarks**: identity systems by branding agencies such as Pentagram, Landor, Wolff Olins, COLLINS, and Interbrand, as well as outstanding logo, wordmark, and brand extension cases collected by Brand New and D&AD.

## Goals

### Form an identity concept from the brand's differentiators
Understand the brand name, business, audience, personality, competitive landscape, and the differences that need to be remembered first, then decide whether to use a wordmark, a symbol, a combination mark, or an app icon. Do not apply the most common graphic metaphor directly from industry keywords.

### Build recognition into simple forms
The identity must create a clear point of memory in its silhouette, letterforms, proportions, or negative space. Complex effects may only serve as application-layer expression; the core identity must remain recognizable in a single color, at small sizes, and against ordinary backgrounds.

### Establish consistent lockup and extension rules
Define the primary mark, horizontal and vertical versions, icon, reversed (knockout) version, clear space, and basic color relationships. Later applications — business cards, social media, packaging, or interfaces — must reuse the same identity assets; do not generate separate versions that are similar but inconsistent.

### Build the core identity as editable vector structures
Image generation may be used for early concept exploration and mood direction, but the final logo, wordmark, and app icons should be rebuilt primarily with the text, custom shapes, and basic geometry of `pptd.md`, ensuring crisp edges, accurate text, and continued editability. Rendered mockups are only for showing applications; they do not replace the identity itself.

## Visual references

The directions below are classified by where the brand identity comes from, not by vague temperaments such as "premium, youthful, tech." First find an explainable identity concept from the name, business, and cultural context, then choose one construction method for the primary mark, and validate single-color, small-size, and horizontal/vertical lockups at the same time.

The entries below are only meant to help you understand what excellent design in this genre might look like; they are not a routing table to be matched item by item, nor must any single one be applied in full. Form your own direction from the user's content, then borrow composition, typography, color, imagery, or material treatment from these entries as needed; reasonable approaches not listed here are also acceptable.

### 01. Custom wordmark

**Applies to**: restaurants and food, fashion, culture, personal brands, and brands whose names are themselves memorable. Start from standard letterforms and modify only 1–3 strokes, joins, or counters that best express the brand's personality; preserve the overall weight and reading rhythm so the name reads correctly from afar, with details revealing themselves up close. **Production**: first validate the wordmark in solid black, then extend it into a set of primary-color and reversed versions; do not pass off an off-the-shelf display font as a logo, and do not distort the letterforms so much that the spelling becomes unclear.

### 02. Modular geometric symbol

**Applies to**: cities, travel, platforms, tech services, and brands that need to extend across many scenarios. Build a decomposable symbol from circles, arcs, rectangles, or polylines, where each module can correspond to a location, function, or brand value; the primary mark, icons, patterns, and motion all reuse the same geometric grammar, with color held to 2–4 stable roles. **Production**: rebuild on a grid and test the scaled-down silhouette, showing horizontal, vertical, and icon-only lockups; do not draw a single complex pattern without lockup rules.

### 03. Place-based object mark

**Applies to**: farms, orchards, lodging, local foods, communities, and cultural spaces. Choose one object that truly belongs to the place or business — terrain, fruit, architecture, or a tool — and compress it into a few shape masses and clear negative space; pair it with a plain wordmark and natural colors drawn from the environment to form an identity that is warm but not clichéd. **Production**: gather real local material before simplifying, avoiding generic combinations of mountains, leaves, and suns; the symbol must hold up even without color.

### 04. Naive stamp-style identity

**Applies to**: parent-child, handcraft, coffee, community, nonprofit, and brands that emphasize human warmth. Use hand-drawn outlines, slight asymmetry, and simple figures of people, animals, or houses, with the wordmark keeping the same stroke character; the whole should read as a small stamp that could be inked or made into a sticker, not a polished character illustration. **Production**: keep a small amount of handmade imperfection, but unify line weight, eyes, and corner rules; do not stack cute elements endlessly, and do not rely on complex expressions for recognition.

### 05. Letterform and negative-space icon

**Applies to**: app icons, professional services, tool products, and brands with long names. Extract a shared skeleton from the initial letter or two key letters, and form a single point of memory through clipped corners, overlap, or negative space; keep the silhouette clear inside square, circular, and browser favicon frames. **Production**: test at 16–32 pixels, small-size print, and on light and dark backgrounds at the same time; details should naturally fall away when scaled down rather than blur into a mush; do not mechanically nest two letters inside each other.

### 06. Cultural imprint and modern seal

**Applies to**: traditional crafts, cultural institutions, tea and spirits, publishing, and brands with historical origins. Extract edges and proportions from real seal carving, ornament, artifact structures, or writing methods, then organize them on a modern grid; the primary mark may retain irregular ink edges, while supporting text uses a clean modern typeface, forming a primary-secondary relationship between history and the contemporary. **Production**: cultural symbols must have clearly traceable sources; avoid cross-regional collage. Provide a flat-color vector version as well, with texture serving only as an application layer.

### 07. Variable identity system

**Applies to**: exhibitions, city projects, content platforms, art institutions, and brands with multiple sections or locations. First fix the invariant identity core — a wordmark skeleton, a frame, a grid, or a symbol — then let color, imagery, and module arrangement vary with each section; versions placed side by side must still read as one brand at a glance. **Production**: show at least the primary mark, two variants, and three application sizes, defining what may change and what may not; variable does not mean redesigning a logo every time.

### 08. Mascot character identity

**Applies to**: parent-child, food, cultural tourism, community, content IP, and brands that need a lasting sense of companionship. Build an original character from the brand's personality and use scenarios: first fix the silhouette, proportions, face, primary color, and signature prop, then extend expressions, poses, and seasonal outfits; the logo and the character may be combined, but each must remain independently recognizable at small sizes. **Production**: complete the standard standing pose, head portrait, and three basic expressions first, then make applications; do not appropriate existing IP, and do not let the character's appearance drift from one generation to the next.

### 09. Supergraphic cropping system

**Applies to**: brands that already have a simple logo and need strong recognition across posters, packaging, social media, and space. Extract an enlargeable supergraphic from one arc, clipped corner, counter, or negative space of the mark, and define rules for rotation, cropping, color layering, and minimum clear space; the main content always forms a foreground-background or framing relationship with the graphic, without infinitely enlarging the logo itself. **Production**: show cropping variations at at least three scales along with misuse examples, keeping color roles consistent; graphic variations must not break the core silhouette or obscure information.

### 10. Packaging-first series identity

**Applies to**: food, beverages, fragrance, beauty, agricultural products, and multi-flavor consumer brands. First fix the positions of the brand zone, category zone, flavor/variant zone, and required regulatory information, then let color, illustration, pattern, or numerals vary with each SKU; a single item must identify its flavor at a glance, while a full shelf row should read as one family. **Production**: first place three SKUs side by side to check the series relationship, then extend to labels and social media; do not regenerate the logo on different packaging, and do not let decoration crowd out ingredients and specifications that must remain accurate.

## Prohibited items
1. Do not use generic leaves, flames, speech bubbles, or letter combinations unrelated to the brand as a stand-in for a creative idea.
2. Do not let the final logo depend on complex gradients, textures, shadows, or details that cannot survive scaling down.
3. Do not generate brand names or wordmarks that are wrong, distorted, or impossible to spell accurately.
4. When the user has already provided a logo, do not regenerate an approximate version, which causes drift in proportions, letterforms, and color.
