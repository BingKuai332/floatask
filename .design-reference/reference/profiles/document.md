# Documents and Editorial Design

The following is a general reference for this genre; user requirements, attachments, and the actual scenario take precedence.

Applies to résumés, reports, manuals, book covers, menus, notices, forms, and other reading-oriented or editorial materials.

**Benchmarks**: outstanding annual reports, consulting reports, and brand manuals; mature editorial design such as Monocle, The New York Times Magazine, and Bloomberg Businessweek; and real-world examples of professional résumés, menus, and public information forms.

## Goals

### Establish a layout system suited to continuous reading
Documents rely more heavily on text and repeated structures than posters do. Determine margins, grid, heading hierarchy, body text width, captions, page numbers, and source-citation rules first, so readers can locate information quickly and keep reading without interruption.

### Adjust density to the document's task
A résumé emphasizes fast scanning and comparison of experience; a report emphasizes argument, evidence, and chapter progression; a manual emphasizes lookup; a menu emphasizes categories and prices; a form emphasizes the order of filling it out. Do not treat every document with a single portfolio-style layout.

### Keep multiple pages consistent without mechanical repetition
Keep typefaces, color palette, grid, and image treatment consistent; covers, chapter pages, body pages, data pages, and closing pages should each carry a different rhythm according to their content, rather than duplicating the same page skeleton.

### Prioritize typography; use images only as needed
Prefer the text, shapes, tables, and charts in `pptd.md` to keep content editable. Search for, generate, or edit images only when the cover, people, case studies, or chapters call for visual material; a text-driven document should not be stuffed with irrelevant images just to look rich.

## Visual references

The following directions correspond to different document tasks such as covers, continuous reading, quick scanning, and form filling. First confirm how the reader will use this document, then choose one layout system and carry it through the cover, table of contents, body, charts, and appendix; do not mistake a single beautiful cover for the design of the whole document.

The following entries are only meant to help you understand what excellent design in this genre might look like. They are not a routing table to be matched item by item, nor is any single one required to be applied in full. Form your own direction from the user's content, then borrow from these entries' composition, typography, color, image, or material treatment as needed; reasonable approaches not listed here are also acceptable.

### 01. Quiet literary cover and interior

**Applies to**: novels, essays, poetry collections, travel writing, and cultural reading. The cover focuses on a single fine-line illustration, a small motif, or a restrained title, with large areas of negative space ordered through a central axis, a border, or top and bottom baselines; the interior uses comfortable line lengths, stable margins, and minimal chapter ornamentation. **Production**: keep the illustration to one or two colors, and use editable fonts for the title and author; the negative space must align with the title, image, and spine position — it must not merely be a lack of content.

### 02. Conceptual photography book cover

**Applies to**: nonfiction, science, humanities, business, and book or report covers with a clear proposition. Turn the subject into a photographic metaphor understandable at a glance — for example, letting an object's silhouette become the image container, or using a conflict of scale to make a point; keep only the title, author, and necessary subtitle on the cover, with the type and image sharing the same visual axis. **Production**: search for or photograph credible material first, then do background removal, masking, and compositing; the metaphor must connect back to the content — do not use surreal effects unrelated to the text.

### 03. Illustrated cultural field guide

**Applies to**: local culture, plants, food, handicrafts, travel guides, and children's knowledge books. Establish a unified illustration language and organize objects, people, and scenes by chapter; the cover can form its identity through geometric assembly or a group portrait, while interior pages use a main image, detail images, captions, and an index to build a searchable layout. **Production**: complete 6–12 base illustration assets and a color palette before expanding into pages; do not change the illustration style every chapter, and do not fill empty space with irrelevant patterns.

### 04. Long-form magazine feature

**Applies to**: personality profiles, brand stories, interviews, and city and lifestyle content. Open with a spread-spanning hero image, a short standfirst, and a large headline; body pages vary among single column, double column, pull quotes, detail photographs, and data side notes; photography follows a consistent logic of shot scale and color grading, with at most one headline typeface and one body typeface each. **Production**: plan the reading rhythm of the whole piece before laying out individual pages, and prefer real, high-quality photography; do not package every paragraph into a card, and do not let pull quotes become denser than the body text.

### 05. Analytical report

**Applies to**: consulting reports, annual reports, research reports, business proposals, and internal retrospectives. The cover and chapter pages establish brand identity; the body forms a repeating reading grammar of "conclusion headline — evidence chart — explanatory text — source"; use strict margins, aligned numerals, and a few semantic colors; data pages may be denser, and transition pages should be quieter. **Production**: keep tables and charts editable, and use images only for cases or chapter context; each page should express one conclusion first — do not use large decorative images to mask thin analysis.

### 06. Professional résumé and portfolio record

**Applies to**: job résumés, academic CVs, personal introductions, and portfolio records of one to several pages. Name, target position, and contact information form the first reading layer; experience is aligned by time, organization, role, and outcome, with skills and education receding to a secondary level; creative industries may add a set of personal colors or thumbnails of representative works, but the body's scanning path must not be interrupted. **Production**: use one column or a clearly primary-secondary two-column layout, and stay within 2 font families and 3 type-size levels; never fabricate experience, and do not use progress bars to disguise skills that cannot be quantified.

### 07. Manuals and how-to guides

**Applies to**: brand manuals, employee handbooks, course handouts, product instructions, and public service guides. Build stable components around the table of contents, chapter numbering, steps, warnings, examples, and index; each spread first presents a task or question, then answers it with short steps, diagrams, and cropped screenshots, while page numbers and chapter colors support quick lookup. **Production**: keep step diagrams, tables, and annotations editable, and standardize the cropping and labeling of screenshots; do not replace operational hierarchy with long continuous paragraphs, and do not let decorative colors carry multiple meanings.

### 08. Menus, forms, and notices

**Applies to**: restaurant menus, registration forms, price lists, daily reminders, announcements, and printable information sheets. Organize content around the user's action first: a menu supports comparing categories and prices, a form supports filling from top to bottom, a notice highlights the event, time, and required action; use rules, indentation, alignment, and local background tints to build structure, with decoration serving only the mood of the setting. **Production**: check type sizes, writing space, and trimming at real print or phone dimensions; never weaken prices, fields, checkboxes, or contact information in pursuit of a portfolio feel.

### 09. Newsprint zine

**Applies to**: cultural periodicals, campus newspapers, independent magazines, event programs, and topical documents that emphasize an on-the-scene feel. Use uncoated-paper tones, narrow body columns, cross-column headlines, page numbers, marginalia, and one or two spot colors, with photographs unified as black-and-white, duotone, or coarse halftone; the cover may be high-density, but the body must return to stable column widths and reading rhythm. **Production**: keep real content, captions, and sources editable, and vary multiple pages only in spreads, spot colors, and image scale; do not fill the layout with dummy text, and do not let photocopy noise damage the body text.

### 10. Archival research dossier

**Applies to**: historical research, case retrospectives, investigation records, curatorial materials, and process-oriented portfolios. Build a three-layer structure of "archive — annotation — conclusion" around the main documents, photographic evidence, timestamps, numbering, maps, or index; scans keep their edges and material quality, while modern commentary uses a strict grid and one marker color to distinguish it from the original material. **Production**: label every archival item with its source, date, and role, and do only necessary cleanup and cropping on images; never fabricate archive numbers, seals, or citations, and do not make every page the same collage wall.

## Prohibited items
1. Do not turn a reading-oriented document into a poster with only large headlines and decorative images, where the body text is hard to read continuously.
2. Do not use overly small body text, runaway line lengths, too many hierarchy levels, or a different font system for every module.
3. Do not let a multi-page document repeatedly change its grid, margins, page numbers, captions, or image style.
4. Do not fabricate experience, data, prices, or contact information for résumés, reports, menus, and forms without user confirmation.
